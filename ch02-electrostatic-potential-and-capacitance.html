1. Electrostatic Potential

Work done by electrostatic force is path independent (conservative force). Hence potential energy
can be defined.

Electric potential (V) at a point: the work done per unit positive test charge in bringing it from infinity
to that point, without acceleration, against the electric field.

V = W / q₀ (SI unit: volt, 1 V = 1 J/C; dimensions [M L² T⁻³ A⁻¹])

Potential difference (V_A − V_B): work done per unit positive charge in moving it from B to A (against
the field).

1 volt: potential difference between two points is 1 V if 1 J of work is done in moving 1 C of charge
between them.

Key points

• Potential is a scalar; potential at infinity is taken as zero.

• Electric field points from high to low potential.

• Relation between E and V: E = −dV/dr. E is the negative potential gradient; unit V/m = N/C.

• Electric field is perpendicular to an equipotential surface and points in the direction of fastest fall of

potential.

2. Potential due to a Point Charge

Derivation. Charge Q at origin. Find V at P, distance r.

Work done in moving a unit positive charge from ∞ to P against the field:

V = −∫(∞ to r) E·dr = −∫(∞ to r) [Q / (4πε₀r²)] dr = −(Q/4πε₀) [−1/r] from ∞ to r

V = Q / (4πε₀ r)

• For Q > 0, V is positive; for Q < 0, V is negative.

• V ∝ 1/r, while E ∝ 1/r². The graph of V against r is a rectangular hyperbola.

3. Potential due to an Electric Dipole

Dipole: charges +q and −q separated by 2a; dipole moment p = q(2a), directed from −q to +q.

Potential at a point (r, θ), r >> a:

Distances: r₁ ≈ r − a cosθ (from +q), r₂ ≈ r + a cosθ (from −q).

V = (q/4πε₀)(1/r₁ − 1/r₂) = (q/4πε₀) · (r₂ − r₁)/(r₁r₂) = (q/4πε₀) · 2a cosθ / (r² − a²cos²θ)

For r >> a:

V = p cosθ / (4πε₀ r²)

Special cases

• Axial line (θ = 0° or 180°): V = ± p / (4πε₀r²)

• Equatorial line (θ = 90°): V = 0, for every point on it. The equatorial plane is an equipotential

surface at zero potential.

Note: for a dipole V ∝ 1/r², whereas for a point charge V ∝ 1/r.

4. Potential due to a System of Charges

By the superposition principle, the potential at a point is the algebraic sum of the potentials due to
individual charges:

V = (1/4πε₀) [q₁/r₁ + q₂/r₂ + ... + qₙ/rₙ]

• For a uniformly charged spherical shell (radius R, charge Q): outside or on the surface, V =

Q/(4πε₀r), as if all the charge were at the centre; inside, V = Q/(4πε₀R) (constant). E = 0 inside.

5. Equipotential Surfaces

Definition: a surface on which the potential has the same value at every point.

Properties

1. No work is done in moving a charge between two points on it (W = qΔV = 0).

2. The electric field is always perpendicular to the surface. Proof: if E had a tangential component, work

would be done in moving a charge along the surface, contradicting constant V.

3. Two equipotential surfaces never intersect (two directions of E at one point would be impossible).

4. Field lines point from higher to lower potential.

5. Where the surfaces are closer together, the field is stronger (E = −dV/dr).

Shapes

• Point charge: concentric spheres.

• Uniform field: parallel planes perpendicular to the field.

• Dipole: complex surfaces; the equatorial plane is at V = 0.

6. Potential Energy of a System of Charges

Two charges: the work done in assembling q₁ and q₂ at separation r₁₂:

U = q₁q₂ / (4πε₀ r₁₂)

Three charges (derivation). Bring q₁ first (no work). Bring q₂: work = q₂ · V₁ = q₁q₂/(4πε₀r₁₂). Bring q₃ in
the field of both: work = q₃(q₁/r₁₃ + q₂/r₂₃)/(4πε₀).

U = (1/4πε₀) [q₁q₂/r₁₂ + q₁q₃/r₁₃ + q₂q₃/r₂₃]

Signs of the charges must be included. U is independent of the order of assembly. Negative U means a
bound (attractive) system.

Potential energy in an external field

• Single charge q at position r: U = qV(r).

• Two charges in an external potential V: U = q₁V(r₁) + q₂V(r₂) + q₁q₂/(4πε₀r₁₂).

Potential energy of a dipole in a uniform field

Torque on dipole: τ = pE sinθ. Work done in rotating it from θ₀ to θ:

W = ∫ pE sinθ dθ = pE (cosθ₀ − cosθ)

Taking θ₀ = 90° (zero of PE, since the net work on the two charges from infinity is zero there):

U = −pE cosθ = −p·E

• Stable equilibrium: θ = 0°, U = −pE (minimum).

• Unstable equilibrium: θ = 180°, U = +pE (maximum).

• Work done to rotate from 0° to 180° = 2pE.

7. Electrostatics of Conductors

1. Inside a conductor, E = 0 (static case). Free electrons rearrange until the net field is zero.

2. At the surface, E is normal to the surface, with E = σ/ε₀ (outward for σ > 0).

3. Net charge resides only on the surface. By Gauss's law: with E = 0 inside, the flux through any

Gaussian surface inside is zero, so the enclosed charge is zero.

4. The potential is constant throughout the volume of a conductor and equals its surface value (E

= −dV/dr = 0). The interior and surface are equipotential.

5. Electrostatic shielding: a cavity inside a conductor is shielded from external fields (E = 0 inside the

cavity, regardless of the outside field). Uses: sensitive instruments enclosed in metal boxes; safety
inside a car or metal cage during lightning.

6. Charge density is greater at sharp points (smaller radius of curvature), so the field there is large.

This causes corona discharge and is the principle of lightning conductors. Also, for two connected
spheres, σ ∝ 1/R.

Proof of E = σ/ε₀ at the surface: take a small pillbox Gaussian surface straddling the surface. Flux only
through the outer face: E·ΔS = σΔS/ε₀, so E = σ/ε₀.

8. Dielectrics and Polarisation

Dielectrics: insulating substances that contain no free charge carriers and can be polarised.

Two types

• Non-polar (e.g. H₂, O₂, N₂, CO₂, benzene): centres of positive and negative charge coincide; no

permanent dipole moment. An external field induces dipoles.

• Polar (e.g. H₂O, HCl, NH₃, CO): centres do not coincide; permanent dipole moment, randomly oriented.

An external field partially aligns them (opposed by thermal agitation).

Polarisation: in an external field E₀, a dielectric develops a net dipole moment; induced surface charges
appear (–σ_p on the face near the positive plate, +σ_p on the other face).

Polarisation P = dipole moment per unit volume. For linear isotropic dielectrics, P = ε₀χₑE (χₑ = electric
susceptibility).

Effect: the induced charges produce a field opposite to E₀, so the net field is reduced:

E = E₀ − E_p = E₀ / K

Dielectric constant (relative permittivity) K = ε_r = E₀/E = C/C₀ (ratio of the capacitance with
dielectric to the capacitance in vacuum; dimensionless; K = 1 for vacuum; K = ∞ for a conductor).

Values: air ≈ 1, paper ≈ 3.7, glass 5–10, water ≈ 80, mica ≈ 6.

9. Capacitors and Capacitance

Capacitor: a system of two conductors separated by an insulator (dielectric), used to store charge and
energy. Charges on the plates: +Q and −Q.

Capacitance: the ratio of charge on either conductor to the potential difference between them.

C = Q / V (SI unit: farad, 1 F = 1 C/V; common: μF = 10⁻⁶ F, nF, pF)

Farad: the capacitance of a capacitor for which a charge of 1 coulomb raises the potential difference by 1
volt.

Depends only on the geometry (plate area, separation) and the medium, and not on Q or V.

Capacitance of an isolated sphere: C = 4πε₀R (in vacuum).

10. Parallel Plate Capacitor

Two plates, each of area A, separation d (d² << A), charge ±Q, surface density σ = Q/A.

Field (derivation). Between the plates (using the field of infinite sheets): E = σ/ε₀ = Q/(ε₀A). Outside the
plates, E ≈ 0. Edge effects ignored.

Potential difference: V = E·d = Qd/(ε₀A)

C = Q/V = ε₀A / d

With a dielectric completely filling the space: E = σ/(Kε₀), so V = Qd/(Kε₀A):

C = Kε₀A / d = K C₀

Ways to increase C: increase A, reduce d, insert a dielectric of high K.

(a) Partially filled with a dielectric slab (thickness t < d)

Field in air gap: E₀ = σ/ε₀; in the slab: E = E₀/K.

V = E₀(d − t) + E₀t/K = E₀[d − t(1 − 1/K)]

C = ε₀A / [d − t(1 − 1/K)]

11. Combination of Capacitors

Series (same charge Q on each; potentials add)

V = V₁ + V₂ + V₃ ⇒ Q/C_s = Q/C₁ + Q/C₂ + Q/C₃

1/C_s = 1/C₁ + 1/C₂ + 1/C₃ (C_s is smaller than the smallest; V divides inversely as C.)

For two: C_s = C₁C₂/(C₁ + C₂).

Parallel (same V across each; charges add)

Q = Q₁ + Q₂ + Q₃ ⇒ C_pV = C₁V + C₂V + C₃V

C_p = C₁ + C₂ + C₃ (C_p is larger than the largest; Q divides in proportion to C.)

12. Energy Stored in a Capacitor

Derivation. Charging from 0 to Q. When charge q is on it, the potential is v = q/C. Work to add dq:

dW = v dq = (q/C) dq

W = ∫(0 to Q) (q/C) dq = Q²/2C

U = ½ CV² = Q²/2C = ½ QV

Energy density (energy per unit volume, using C = ε₀A/d, V = Ed, volume = Ad):

U = ½ (ε₀A/d)(E²d²) = ½ ε₀E² (Ad)

u = ½ ε₀E² (with dielectric: u = ½ Kε₀E²)

This shows energy is stored in the electric field in the region between the plates.

Sharing of charge between two capacitors

Capacitors C₁ (at V₁) and C₂ (at V₂) are connected by a wire. Charge is conserved, and they reach a
common potential:

V = (C₁V₁ + C₂V₂) / (C₁ + C₂)

Loss of energy: ΔU = [C₁C₂ / 2(C₁ + C₂)] (V₁ − V₂)²

This is always positive. The energy is lost as heat and radiation in the connecting wire. If V₁ = V₂, there is
no loss.

13. Van de Graaff Generator

Purpose: to build up very high potential differences (a few million volts), used to accelerate charged
particles (protons, deuterons) for nuclear experiments.

Principle: (i) charge resides on the outer surface of a hollow conductor, however much charge is already
on it; (ii) the action of sharp points (corona discharge).

Construction: a large hollow metal sphere (spherical shell) supported on insulating columns. An
insulating belt runs over two pulleys, one at the sphere's centre and one at the base. A comb of sharp
needles (spray comb) near the lower pulley is connected to a high-voltage source (~10⁴ V); a second
comb (collecting comb) inside the sphere is connected to the shell.

Working: the lower comb sprays positive charge on the belt by corona discharge. The belt carries it up
into the sphere. The collecting comb picks up the charge, which moves to the outer surface of the shell.
Continued operation raises the potential of the shell until it is limited by leakage into the air (breakdown of
air).

Limit: the potential is limited to a few million volts by ionisation and leakage of charge into the
surrounding air. Enclosing the sphere in a chamber of compressed gas (high dielectric strength) increases
the limit.
